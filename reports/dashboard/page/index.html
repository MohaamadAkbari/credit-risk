The eight tables and how they join

The main table has one row per application. Two related tables, credits at other lenders and earlier Home Credit applications, join on the applicant ID; four monthly or instalment tables hang off those credits and loans. Kaggle's test file has the same columns as the main table but no outcome. Coverage is the share of labelled applicants with at least one row.

What the columns are

Each column belongs to a business family (its group) and holds one kind of value. The kind decides how a column is read and charted: amounts on a log scale, day counts as years before the application, scores as deciles, categories as bars sorted by risk.

Loan size and length

Default rate in each fifth of applicants. Risk is highest for mid-sized loans and loans of 15 to 22 months, and climbs steeply when the loan exceeds the price of the goods. These shapes are why profit is counted per unit of money lent, and why loan amount and term get no forced one-way (monotone) rule in the model.

Missing data carries information

For each main-table column with gaps: the default rate when the value is missing and when it is present, with the share missing on the right. The 47 building-statistics columns are missing together, so one stands for them. The third external score and the bureau-enquiry counts are missing almost only for thin-file applicants, so their gap is really "no credit history".

Strongest single signals

Information value (IV) of the 25 strongest model inputs, measured on the training fold only. A common reading: under 0.02 none, 0.02 to 0.1 weak, 0.1 to 0.3 medium, over 0.3 strong; the dashed lines mark 0.1 and 0.3. The external scores lead; the bureau and earlier-loan tables add many weaker but distinct signals.

Thin-file applicants

Applicants with no record in the credit bureau. They are riskier, younger, and lack most bureau-derived information, so the model will rank them less well than others.

Fairness: gender and age

Gender and age are left out of the model's inputs but kept to audit its decisions. Default rates differ by both, and the other columns can largely reconstruct them, so fairness must be measured on outcomes. Every cell below is large enough to measure (point at a cell for its 95% margin).

How different is a new population?

Kaggle's unlabelled test file stands in for future applicants. A model can tell it apart from the training file almost perfectly, mostly through shorter loans and bureau enquiries recorded in different time windows. It is used to rehearse drift monitoring, not to measure accuracy.

Definitions and methods
Default
TARGET = 1: a late payment beyond an unpublished threshold on one of the first instalments. Payment difficulty, not a write-off.
Weighted by loan amount
Each loan counts by its amount, so the rate answers "what share of the money lent went to applicants with payment difficulty".
Thin file
No record in the credit-bureau table.
Information value (IV)
How strongly one input separates defaulters from others, computed over deciles of the input with missing values as their own group, on the training fold only.
Days and months
All dates are counts relative to the application day; the dictionary shows them as years or months before it. 365243 means "no date" and is excluded.
Normalised scores
External scores and building statistics are scaled to 0–1 by the data provider; their units are unknown.
Ranges
1st to 99th percentile with the median, so a few extreme values do not hide the typical range.
Histograms
20 equal ranges between the 0.5th and 99.5th percentile (log-spaced for amounts); values beyond fall in the end ranges.
Break-even risk 16.7%
With the placeholder economics (12% margin, 60% loss given default), approving pays below this probability of default.
Scope
Descriptive figures use all labelled applications, as in the EDA; the IV values use the training fold only, so the held-out test set stays unseen.
